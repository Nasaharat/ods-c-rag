# Abstracting and Coded Data Items

## What an abstract is

An abstract is a structured summary of one cancer case, pulled from the patient's medical record. Instead of keeping the whole chart, the registrar records the specific data items that registries and researchers need, in a standard coded form so that records can be compared across facilities and over time.

## Core data items

A complete abstract captures several groups of information.

Patient identification and demographics, such as date of birth, sex, and race and ethnicity, describe who the patient is.

Tumor identification describes the cancer itself: the primary site, the histology, the behavior, the grade, and the date of diagnosis.

Stage and extent of disease record how far the cancer had spread at diagnosis.

First course of treatment records what was done to treat the cancer initially, such as surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, or hormone therapy.

Follow-up records outcomes over time, including recurrence and vital status.

## Primary site and histology

The primary site is the organ or tissue where the cancer started. This matters because a cancer is always classified by where it began, not where it later spread. A cancer that starts in the colon and spreads to the liver is still coded as a colon primary.

Histology describes what the cancer cells look like under the microscope, in other words the type of cell the tumor is made of.

The International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, known as ICD-O, provides the coding system. It uses a topography code for the primary site and a morphology code for the histology and behavior.

## Behavior

Behavior describes how the tumor acts. The main categories are benign, uncertain or borderline, in situ, and malignant. In situ tumors are confined to their layer of origin. Malignant tumors can invade and spread. Most reportable solid tumors are malignant primaries.

## Grade

Grade describes how abnormal the cancer cells look compared with normal cells. Well differentiated cells still resemble normal tissue and tend to grow more slowly. Poorly differentiated or undifferentiated cells look very abnormal and tend to behave more aggressively. Grade is recorded separately from stage.

## Data quality

Good registry data is complete, accurate, and timely. Registries run consistency checks, called edits, to catch impossible or contradictory combinations, such as a site and histology that cannot occur together. Careful abstracting and adherence to the current coding rules are what make the data trustworthy for research.
