An Overview of Cancer Staging Concepts

Staging describes how far a cancer has spread at the time of diagnosis.
Consistent staging lets clinicians compare cases, choose treatment, and study
outcomes across large groups of patients. Registries record stage using more
than one system because each serves a different purpose.

The TNM system describes three things about a solid tumor. T stands for the
size or extent of the primary tumor. N stands for whether and how far the
cancer has spread to nearby lymph nodes. M stands for whether the cancer has
spread, or metastasized, to distant parts of the body. Each letter is given a
number that reflects increasing extent, and the combination maps to an overall
stage group, usually written with Roman numerals from I through IV.

Higher numbers generally mean more extensive disease. A small tumor confined to
its origin sits at the low end, while disease that has spread to distant organs
sits at the high end. Some cancers also use the value 0 for in situ disease,
meaning abnormal cells that have not yet invaded surrounding tissue.

Summary staging is a simpler scheme that groups cases into broad categories
such as in situ, localized, regional, and distant. Localized means the cancer
is limited to the organ where it began. Regional means it has spread to nearby
tissue or lymph nodes. Distant means it has reached far parts of the body.
Summary stage is useful for population-level statistics where the fine detail
of TNM is not needed.

A key idea for registry work is timing. The stage recorded at diagnosis is
based on information available before treatment begins, combined with findings
from any initial surgery. Later progression of the disease does not change the
stage that was assigned at diagnosis. Keeping that timing rule straight is a
common source of coding errors for people learning to abstract.

Because different systems coexist, an abstractor must know which system a given
data item is asking for and apply the matching rules. Mixing rules from
different systems is one of the easiest mistakes to make and one that edit
checks are designed to catch.
