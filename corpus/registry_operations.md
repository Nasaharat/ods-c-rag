# Cancer Registry Operations

## What a cancer registry is

A cancer registry is a system that collects, stores, manages, and analyzes data about people diagnosed with cancer. The data supports research, public health planning, and patient care. A registry is the foundation for measuring cancer incidence and for survival analysis, because it tracks who was diagnosed, with what, and what happened next.

## Two main types of registry

A hospital registry, also called a facility registry, tracks the cancer patients seen at one facility. It supports patient follow-up, quality of care review, and program accreditation.

A central registry, also called a population-based registry, covers everyone diagnosed within a defined geographic area, such as a state. Its job is surveillance: counting cases across a whole population so that incidence and trends can be measured without bias toward any single hospital.

## The reference date

The reference date is the date a registry begins including cases. Every eligible case diagnosed on or after that date is part of the registry. Cases diagnosed before the reference date are generally not included unless the registry runs a special project to add them.

## Reportability

A reportable list defines which conditions must be entered into the registry. In the United States, reportable conditions are generally malignant primary cancers, including invasive and in situ tumors, plus certain benign and borderline tumors of the brain and central nervous system. Common skin cancers such as basal cell and squamous cell carcinoma are usually not reportable, except at a few specific sites. The exact list is set by standard-setting organizations, not by individual registrars.

## Organizations that set standards

Several organizations define how cancer data is collected and coded so that registries across the country stay consistent. These include the North American Association of Central Cancer Registries (NAACCR), the National Cancer Institute SEER Program, the CDC National Program of Cancer Registries (NPCR), the Commission on Cancer (CoC) of the American College of Surgeons, and the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC). Because data standards change over time, registries maintain procedure manuals and follow the current rules for the diagnosis year being coded.

## The registrar's workflow

The basic workflow is casefinding, then abstracting, then follow-up. Casefinding identifies reportable cases from sources such as pathology reports and admission records. Abstracting summarizes each case into a structured record. Follow-up keeps the record current with later treatment and outcome information.
