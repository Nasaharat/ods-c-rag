# Cancer Staging Basics

## What staging measures

Stage describes how far a cancer has spread at the time of diagnosis. It is one of the most important pieces of information in a cancer record because it guides treatment decisions and is a strong predictor of outcome. Two cancers of the same type can behave very differently depending on stage.

## The TNM system

The TNM system, maintained by the American Joint Committee on Cancer, describes a solid tumor using three letters.

T describes the primary tumor, usually its size or how far it has grown into nearby tissue.

N describes whether the cancer has reached nearby, or regional, lymph nodes.

M describes whether the cancer has spread to distant parts of the body, which is called distant metastasis.

Each letter is given a number that reflects increasing extent. The T, N, and M values are then combined into an overall stage group, usually written as Stage 0, I, II, III, or IV. Higher stage groups mean more extensive disease.

## Clinical versus pathologic stage

Clinical stage is assigned before treatment, based on physical exam, imaging, and biopsy. It is what is known about the cancer from the outside.

Pathologic stage is assigned after surgery, based on what the pathologist finds when examining the tissue that was removed. Pathologic stage is generally more precise because it is based on direct measurement of the tumor and the lymph nodes.

Both can be recorded for the same patient, and they do not always match.

## Summary Stage

Summary Stage is a simpler scheme used in population surveillance. It places a cancer into one of a few broad categories based on how far it has spread from where it started.

In situ means the abnormal cells are still confined to the layer of tissue where they began and have not invaded.

Localized means the cancer is limited to the organ where it started.

Regional means it has spread to nearby tissue or regional lymph nodes.

Distant means it has spread to organs or lymph nodes far from the primary site.

Unknown is used when there is not enough information to assign a category.

## In situ versus invasive

The line between in situ and invasive matters a great deal. In situ disease has not broken through into surrounding tissue and is highly treatable. Once a cancer invades beyond its layer of origin, it is considered invasive and can potentially spread.
